How it works:

Lunar eclipses are in. When the moon passes through the planet's shadow, the shadow's curved edge creeps across the disc, and at totality the moon turns a dim copper red instead of vanishing. The moonlight on the night-side land, trees and sea dims and reddens with it. Committed and pushed. I've seen it in test captures only, not live in the game.

How it works:
- The shadow: from the moon, the planet covers the sun completely, with a thin band (the penumbra) where only part of the sun is hidden. Light falls off smoothly across that band. Inside the full shadow, the only light is sunlight bent through the planet's atmosphere. That light is reddest at the centre and brighter toward the shadow's edge, which is why real eclipsed moons glow copper.
- Brightness in totality: a real eclipsed moon is about a thousandth as bright as a full moon, which at the game's fixed night exposure is black. I set it to the level a dark-adapted eye sees, as I did for the moonlight itself. The moon is clearly visible but dark red, and the ground in the test view goes about 8x darker.
- How often eclipses happen: the moon's orbit used to stay fixed in space and the game's sun doesn't move through the year, so you'd have got either an eclipse every full moon or none ever. The orbit is now tilted 15° to the sun's plane and slowly turns, once every 346.6 game days. That is Earth's "eclipse year", the time the moon's orbit takes to line up with the sun again. So eclipses now come in seasons about 173 game days apart, as on Earth. Each season usually has one total eclipse, about 0.6 days of totality inside an event lasting just over a day, plus a partial or a faint penumbral one. Earth's moon is tilted only 5°, but this moon is much closer, so the planet's shadow looks about six times wider from it and 5° would eclipse every full moon.
